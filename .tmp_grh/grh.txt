                    0. Statement from the author
   The main purpose of this preliminary report is to begin looking for some
of the sources, provenance, and mathematical history behind the proposed
proof of the generalized Riemann hypothesis announced by Levent Alpöge
using a Claude model [Alp26] on November 20, 2026. The mathematics
involved reflects many lifetimes of work, so I would like to recognize as
many as possible of the people whose work may have contributed to it: the
mathematicians working today, our teachers, and all of our predecessors.
This is only a beginning, and I apologize for all the people, papers, and other
contributions I may have missed.
   The ideas used in modern mathematical proofs inevitably come from
people, and I think that sharing them carries obligations to those people; see
Harris [Har26a] for a great account of this notion of mathematics as a “gift
economy”. I also wonder what becomes of the labor and generosity behind
the ideas, and who is expected to do the further work of understanding any
proof announcement. While I share Michael Harris’s [Har26b] objection to
mathematicians acting as unpaid labor for AI companies, I am attempting to
interpret this AI artifact because I want to recognize some of the mathematical
origins of [Alp26] and the credit that is due to the people involved (without
claiming to offer a complete history or to settle any questions of ownership).

I hope we will continue to remember and celebrate the people whose ideas

   The scale of the material involved in [Alp26] makes this an unusual
exercise in exposition: the Lean code has roughly 17 million lines of code,
while Alpöge [Alp26, p. 2, footnote 2] describes an AI-generated exposition
of more than 250,000 pages that he chose not to release. Neither offers a
practicable prospect of reading the argument from beginning to end in its
present form. As such, I have also used AI to begin writing this report: I
prompted GPT 6 Pro to digest the Lean code on Alpöge’s website, infer a
human-readable mathematical proof, to search the literature carefully for
earlier sources and analogues of the ideas in the argument, and then to
produce a written summary. These prompts were an attempt to obtain
something that people could begin to read, discuss, and eventually improve.
I then spent November 20, 2026 rewriting the AI output into the current
form. Given the impossibility of any human checking natural language text
against 17 million lines of Lean code in a short timeframe, I cannot claim
that this summary is a faithful translation of the Lean code in [Alp26],
but an incredible collective effort by the Mathlib and Lean communities
[Lea26, Sut26] already resulted (after just one day!) in the verification of
the Lean proof of the generalized Riemann hypothesis using independent
implementations of the Lean kernel and in the verification that the Lean
definitions agree with standard mathematical definitions. I also apologize to
the reader for the condensed and imperfect exposition here, as most of the
text from Section 2 onwards was largely written by AI.
   Part of the pleasure of mathematics, for me, is that an idea can pass
from one person to another and take on a life that neither could have
anticipated. There is an art in making that possible, through the patient
work of explanation and the equally patient work of learning, rooted in
the earliest oral traditions of human culture. I hope that mathematicians
will come to understand both proposed proofs of the generalized Riemann
hypothesis that were announced on November 20, 2026,1 discover what is
new in them, and explain what further questions they make possible. I would
be very glad if this small report helped someone write such an account. Such
understanding should be available to anyone who wishes to learn, regardless
of access to powerful AI systems.

   To see why a bound for Gψ can still detect zeros of the individual L-
functions, notice that Gχ (x) = Gχ (x). Since Xψ is stable under complex
conjugation, the real parts can be omitted from the sum in Equation 1.1.
At a zero ρ of one of the L(s, χ) with χ ∈ Xψ in the critical strip, the
residue of the Mellin transform of Gψ is therefore 1/(ρ(1 − ρ)) times the
sum of the multiplicities of ρ in the L(s, χ) with χ ∈ Xψ , weighted by the
positive coefficients in Equation 1.1; see Equation 2.3. The sum of these
weighted multiplicities is positive, so ρ remains a pole even when several of
the L-functions vanish there.

automorphic forms, Galois representations, Arakelov geometry, and integral

1.3. How automorphic periods give bounds for Gψ . Why should au-
tomorphic periods enter a question about the sums Gχ ? The logarithmic
derivatives L ′ (s, χ)/L(s, χ) in Section 1.2 already have a classical connection
with periods of abelian varieties with complex multiplication. Chowla and
Selberg [CS49, §4] expressed periods of CM elliptic curves through gamma
values. Anderson [And82, Theorem 2.1] extended this relation to abelian va-
rieties with CM by an abelian extension of Q, expressing their periods, up to
algebraic factors, through combinations of logarithmic derivatives of Dirichlet
L-functions at 0. The functional equation for Dirichlet L-functions then
relates the logarithmic derivatives for the odd characters in these formulas
to values of L ′ /L at 1.
   In [Alp26], CM abelian varieties enter through the theta functions used in
the constructions below. Their periods compare algebraic differentials with
the analytic coordinates in which the theta functions are written. These
factors enter the normalization and archimedean estimates for automorphic
periods, which are integrals of automorphic forms on unitary groups. The
normalized automorphic periods take values in a number field, so their
valuations at finite places can also be studied.
   The lower bounds for these finite valuations involve prime powers satisfying
conditions on Frobenius elements. After expanding those conditions in
Dirichlet characters, [Alp26] obtains expressions involving the sums Sχ from

Automorphic periods and theta lifts. The unitary groups in [Alp26] are defined
over the maximal totally real subfield E+ of an abelian CM field E containing
the field cut out by ψ. For a nondegenerate Hermitian E-space V, write
U(V) for its unitary group over E+ . Using AF and AF,f for the adeles and
finite adeles of a number field F, write [G] := G(E+ )\G(AE+ ) for an algebraic
group G over E+ .
   On these unitary groups, [Alp26] begins with a pairing of cusp forms f
on [U(X0 )] and g on [U(Y0 )], for an inclusion of Hermitian spaces X0 ⊂ Y0
with X0 totally definite. It is a Bessel period of the kind studied by Gan,
Gross, and Prasad [GGP12, §12 and §23]: [Alp26] first integrates g over the
adelic quotient of a unipotent subgroup against the conjugate of a character
to obtain a Fourier coefficient, then pairs its restriction to [U(X0 )] with f.
For the comparison at finite primes, [Alp26] chooses models of the spaces
of automorphic forms over a number field K and normalizes the periods to
obtain K-bilinear pairings, with the choices described in Section 4.1.
   To obtain lower bounds for the valuations of these normalized periods at
finite primes of K, [Alp26] repeatedly applies the theta correspondence of
Weil [Wei64] and Howe [How79]. Integration against a theta kernel carries
an automorphic form from one unitary group to another. Following both
arguments of the pairing gives two sequences of m theta lifts, on groups
U(Xj ) and U(Yj ) for 0 ≤ j ≤ m. Every Xj is totally definite; the signatures
of Yj vary until Ym is also totally definite. Kudla’s [Kud84] seesaw identities
relate the initial Bessel period to the pairings after these theta lifts, which
include Fourier–Jacobi periods before ending in a restriction pairing for
U(Ym ) ⊂ U(Xm ).
   The successive theta lifts contribute terms to the lower bounds for these
finite valuations. The normalizations introduce correction terms, and the
theta lifts also change the Petersson norms of the automorphic forms and
the dimensions of their spaces. To keep track of the norms, [Alp26] uses
the formulas of Rallis [Ral87] and Gan, Qiu, and Takeda [GQT14, §11],

which express Petersson norms of theta lifts through special values of L-
functions and local integrals. For the dimensions and multiplicities, [Alp26]
uses Weyl’s [Wey46] dimension formula and weighted counts of Hermitian
lattices of the kind appearing in Siegel’s [Sie35] mass formula.
   With the CM periods included in the normalization, [Alp26] combines the
formulas for Petersson norms, dimensions, and weighted counts of Hermitian
lattices. The resulting expression agrees with the main term subtracted
in Proposition 3.1. This agreement is the identity in Equation 4.5. After
subtracting the main term, [Alp26] bounds the remaining terms to estimate
Gψ .

Lattices of automorphic forms. To compare finite valuations with archimedean
norms, [Alp26] works with lattices over the ring of integers OK of K. Since
U(X0 ) is compact at every real place, its irreducible representations at those
places are specified by integral highest weights. For highest weights α,
[Alp26] uses an OK -lattice Mα of algebraic automorphic forms on U(X0 ), in
the sense of Gross [Gro99]. These algebraic automorphic forms take values
in the algebraic representation of ResE+ /Q U(X0 ) with highest weights α. The
integral structure in this representation and the additional local conditions
on the automorphic forms are described in Sections 4.1 and 4.4. On the
other side of the pairing, downward theta lifts from U(Ym ) give a lattice MW
in a finite-dimensional K-space W of test automorphic forms on [U(Y0 )], with
test vectors at the real places and local conditions at finite places chosen in
[Alp26].

index sublattice Mα′ ⊆ Mα and a fractional ideal aα such that Pα (Mα′ , MW ) ⊆

covolumes of Mα , [Alp26] also uses holomorphic automorphic forms as

sections of bundles on a moduli scheme M of principally polarized abelian
varieties with a compatible OE -action, given signature, and level. The bundles
are constructed from invariant differentials of the universal abelian scheme
over M, and their integral sections are studied on arithmetic compactifications,
as in Faltings and Chai [FC90]. The arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel formulas of
Gillet and Soulé [GS88] and Zhang [Zha95] relate the covolumes of lattices of
integral sections to arithmetic self-intersection numbers. To compare these
numbers with heights of points, [Alp26] uses Zhang’s [Zha95] inequalities for
successive minima and Moriwaki’s [Mor09] extension of Zhang’s criterion for
small sections, developed using ideas from Yuan [Yua08]. The contributions
of finite primes to those heights require control of the integral modules
of invariant differentials. For that control, [Alp26] uses Fargues’s [Far11,
§5.3.2] estimates for Hodge–Tate maps and the prismatic Dieudonné theory
of Anschütz and Le Bras [ALB23]. Section 4.3 explains how these results
give the required covolume bounds.
   Even with covolume bounds for Nα , the norms of the period maps bα
alone cannot bound the covolume of C: if all their images lay in one line,
they would say nothing about the other directions in W ∨ . So [Alp26] proves
that the period functionals span nearly all of W ∨ , and still do so after small
losses of dimension in their domains. The restrictions arise from the Harder–
Narasimhan filtration for Hermitian lattices, developed by Stuhler [Stu76]
and Grayson [Gra84] from the construction for vector bundles by Harder and
Narasimhan [HN75]. Since its successive quotients have decreasing slopes,
[Alp26] can retain the parts of Nα above a chosen slope threshold and use
Bost’s [Bos01, §4.1.4] inequality to control the slopes of their images in C.
The bounds for inner products also measure how much of W ∨ these restricted
images can miss: by Lemma 4.2, the codimension of their span is bounded in
terms of the dimensions removed from the domains. Varying the threshold
then gives the lower bound for µ(C) in Proposition 4.3.

Choosing complex places avoiding three-term arithmetic progressions. The
lower bounds for valuations of period values involve powers of rational primes
satisfying Frobenius conditions. By character orthogonality, [Alp26] expresses
these conditions through Dirichlet characters and obtains terms Gχ (at /wj,ρ ),
as in Proposition 3.1. The positive parameters at are attached to a set T
of complex places of E contributing to the valuations. For U(Xj ) at the real
place corresponding to a complex place ρ of E, the jth theta lift adds a
block of Harish–Chandra parameters centred at −wj,ρ . The positive integers
wj,ρ thus determine these added blocks, as in Section 4.1. By choosing wj,ρ
and averaging over subgroups of Gal(E/Q), [Alp26] retains the characters
inducing powers of ψ with enough of their arguments close to x to recover
Gψ (x).
   The complex places at which this choice is made form a set T2 . It must
be large, but the valuation bounds also lose precision when too many of
its places lie in a short segment of an orbit of Frobenius. To satisfy both

requirements, [Alp26] adapts Behrend’s [Beh46] construction of large sets of
integers not containing any nonconstant three-term arithmetic progressions.
The version used in Section 4.6 limits each relevant intersection to at most
two places. At the remaining complex places, the arguments at /wj,ρ are
larger, and [Alp26] bounds their contributions using regions without zeros of
the Dirichlet L-functions.


Hecke eigenvalue congruences and Galois cohomology. The formulas for Pe-
tersson norms are applied to automorphic forms with given Hecke eigenvalues,
so [Alp26] projects onto sums of simultaneous Hecke eigenspaces. Since a
decomposition over K need not split an OK -lattice, these projections can
introduce denominators and cancel part of the divisibility of the period
values. To bound the denominators introduced by projection onto Hecke
eigenspaces, [Alp26] studies congruences between Hecke eigenvalues using
Galois representations.
   Base change first transfers the representations of the definite unitary
groups to general linear groups over AE . For the regular algebraic polarized
cuspidal constituents, after the twists described in Section 4.2, the results of
Clozel, Harris, and Labesse [CHL11], Shin [Shi11], and Chenevier and Har-
ris [CH13] attach representations of Gal(E/E) whose Frobenius eigenvalues
are determined by the Hecke eigenvalues in the normalization of Theorem 4.1.
Congruences between the automorphic forms can then be studied through
extension classes retained by integral lattices in these Galois representations.
   The one-dimensional summands produced by theta lifting contribute
groups Sℓ (E, j), for rational primes ℓ and integers j ≥ 1. These are kernels
of restriction from first Galois cohomology, unramified away from ℓ, to the
completions of E above ℓ, with coefficients in the Tate twist Qℓ /Zℓ (−j). The
duality theorems of Tate [Tat63] and Poitou [Poi67] relate their orders to
second Galois cohomology, with corrections from local and global invariants.
To bound part of this second cohomology, [Alp26] compares deformations
of Galois representations with congruences between automorphic forms, fol-
lowing Wiles [Wil95], Taylor and Wiles [TW95], and Clozel, Harris, and
Taylor [CHT08]. For certain reducible three-dimensional Galois representa-
tions, the deformation argument also involves Bloch–Kato Selmer groups of
two-dimensional modular representations and their quadratic twists; to show
that these Selmer groups vanish, [Alp26] uses Kato’s [Kat04] Euler system.
   These bounds must allow the highest weights, and hence the range of Tate
twists, to grow. So [Alp26] also varies E through cyclotomic towers, using
Iwasawa’s [Iwa59] theory and Ferrero and Washington’s [FW79] vanishing
of the µ-invariant for abelian fields. Coarser bounds are needed at certain
exceptional twists. The freedom to choose intervals in Proposition 1.2 is used
here: [Alp26] chooses an interval on which every contributing exceptional
index is small relative to x. The resulting bound for the orders of Sℓ (E, j) is
recorded in Proposition 5.3.

         2. Detecting zeros from sums over prime powers
   The estimate for Gψ sought in [Alp26] has two limitations: it holds only
on certain intervals [Y, Y 3/2 ], with Y arbitrarily large, and even there it gives
only an upper bound. To see why this suffices for GRH, consider the effect
of a zero of an L-function on the Mellin transform of Gψ . The positive
coefficients in the linear combination Gψ of the corrected sums Gχ prevent
contributions from a shared zero from canceling. By differentiating the
Mellin transform many times, [Alp26] detects a pole using the upper bound
for Gψ on a single interval. The freedom to choose these intervals is useful
in Section 6, where [Alp26] chooses auxiliary number fields and weights of
automorphic representations subject to several arithmetic conditions.
   For this part of the argument, the important feature of Equation 1.1 is
the positivity of its coefficients. The nonvanishing criterion can therefore
be stated for a more general linear combination. Let X be a finite family
of primitive Dirichlet characters, possibly including 1. For positive real

4. Bounding sums over prime powers with automorphic periods
   After the character calculation in Section 3, the remaining problem is

4.1. Increasing divisibility of period values by repeated theta lifts.
Before describing the Bessel and Fourier–Jacobi pairings used in the succes-
sive theta lifts, it helps to recall where the theta correspondence came from.
Its underlying representations grew out of questions in quantum mechanics:
a linear change of position and momentum preserving the commutation
relations should, when possible, be realized by a unitary operator. Van
Hove [vH51] had already studied this problem for canonical transformations.
In the real case, Segal [Seg59] recognized the resulting projective represen-
tation, and Shale [Sha62] developed the theory in his study of the linear
symmetries of free boson fields. In his later account, Segal [Seg63, pp. 31–33]
explained the relation to quantum mechanics and to van Hove’s work.
   The passage to number theory required an extension to local fields and
adeles. In making this extension, Weil [Wei64, pp. 143–144] sought to under-
stand the modularity of theta series and the appearance of symplectic groups
in Siegel’s theory of quadratic forms. He explicitly credited Segal [Seg63]
for the principle of his existence proof and for the starting point in locally
compact abelian groups. Howe [How79] then organized the representation
theory around reductive dual pairs: two reductive groups acting as mutual
centralizers in a symplectic group. Restricting the Weil representation to such
a pair made it possible to pass between representations of its two members.

norms and dimensions of these spaces.

4.3. Obtaining bounds for lattices of automorphic forms. The maps
bα : Nα → C compare lattices, so their norms and denominators are only
part of the comparison. To bound the mean slope of C, [Alp26] also needs
bounds for the covolumes of the initial lattices Mα as levels and highest
weights vary. For this purpose, [Alp26] uses holomorphic automorphic forms
on auxiliary unitary groups and their realization as sections of bundles
on unitary moduli schemes. The argument calls for integral sections with
controlled archimedean norms, and for sections that do not vanish at specified
points. Figure 2 records the results from algebraic and arithmetic geometry
used here.
Automorphic bundles on unitary moduli schemes. Fix the dimension, the
action of OE , its signature, and the level structure in one of the moduli prob-
lems used in [Alp26], and write M for the scheme parametrizing principally
polarized abelian varieties with these data. The signature determines the
dimensions of the eigenspaces for OE in the tangent space of each abelian
variety. Let π : A → M be the universal abelian scheme, with zero section
e. Its Hodge bundle ω := e∗ Ω1A/M is the bundle of invariant differentials
on the fibres of π. The OE -action decomposes ω into eigensubbundles after
extending the field of definition so that the embeddings of E split. For general
highest weights, [Alp26] also uses flag varieties of the Hodge eigensubbundles
and their duals. A point of such a flag variety specifies a filtration with
one-dimensional successive quotients.
   To apply intersection theory to these bundles, [Alp26] works on compactifi-
cations of M and the associated flag varieties. Ash, Mumford, Rapoport, and
Tai [AMRT75] developed toroidal compactifications of arithmetic quotients

[Alp26] also extends M and its bundles over rings of integers or their local-

section is checked at all finite primes. For the Siegel moduli schemes of princi-
pally polarized abelian varieties with level structure, Faltings and Chai [FC90,

tions to semi-abelian varieties, which are extensions of abelian varieties by

algebraic tori. In [Alp26], the boundary charts on compactifications of M
allow cusp forms to be treated as sections vanishing along the boundary.
On the associated flag varieties, the highest weights specify tensor powers
of the quotient lines and their duals. The degrees of these line bundles
enter the bounds for covolumes of lattices of holomorphic automorphic forms.
The argument uses resolution and principalisation in characteristic zero,

   For sections of automorphic bundles on a compactification of M, there are
two questions to answer.
     • First, how large are the spaces of sections?
     • Second, can a section be chosen nonzero at a given point?
For the first question, [Alp26] uses the theorems of Kawamata [Kaw82] and

with effective generation of sections of the kind proved by Angehrn and
Siu [AS95], which bounds the positivity needed to obtain a section that does
not vanish at that point. Together, these results are used in [Alp26] to bound
the dimensions of spaces of sections, the orders of vanishing of sections, and
the covolumes of the integral lattices of sections.

4.5. Recovering the main term from normalized automorphic peri-
ods. To bound A(a, t, w) − M(a, w), [Alp26] combines the bounds for the
norms of bα with those for the valuations of Pα (f, g). Equation 3.4 expresses
the difference A(a, t, w) − M(a, w) in terms of the corrected sums Gχ . A
bound for A(a, t, w) alone would not suffice. The bounds for finite valuations
of period values and for archimedean norms in [Alp26] recover M(a, w) with
exactly the constants in that equation, leaving an error small enough for the
proposed proof.
   The extension from CM elliptic curves to abelian varieties in Ander-
son [And82] drew on Shimura’s [Shi79] relations between periods, together
with calculations of periods of Fermat curves by Weil [Wei76] and by Rohrlich
in the appendix to Gross’s [Gro78] paper. Anderson [And82, §4] also formu-
lated and proved a result of Deligne, unpublished at the time, relating CM
periods to gamma values. The algebraic factors left undetermined by these
formulas also matter in [Alp26], because they affect the finite valuations of
the normalized automorphic periods.
48                               ROBIN ZHANG

     Accounting for these factors requires more than their algebraicity. The
argument also needs to know how the periods change under Galois conju-
gation. For this, [Alp26] uses reciprocity for elliptic curves with complex
multiplication: the conjugate elliptic curve and the action on its torsion are
described through the Artin map. This is the elliptic case of the theory
of Shimura and Taniyama [ST61]; see Milne [Mil07, Theorem 3.13] for a
statement in terms of complex uniformization. The algebraic factors in
the formulas for CM periods can also have denominators at finite places.
To control these, [Alp26] uses the integrality of suitable modified values of
abelian L-functions at negative integers over totally real fields, proved by
Deligne and Ribet [DR80]. The reciprocity law and the integrality of the
modified L-values control the algebraic factors in the expressions for CM
periods; identities involving only absolute values would not determine the
valuations of these factors at finite places.
     The comparison of periods also involves dimensions and multiplicities,
because [Alp26] averages the periods over spaces of automorphic forms.
The dimensions of these spaces vary with the highest weights, and the
multiplicities of their automorphic representations affect the average as well.
To compute the dimensions and multiplicities, [Alp26] uses Weyl’s [Wey46]
formulas for dimensions of representations at infinity together with the mass
formulas associated with Siegel [Sie35]. In Siegel’s theory, weighted counts
of lattices were related to products of local densities. Together with the
Chowla–Selberg formula, the dimension and mass formulas are singled out in
[Alp26] in its explanation of the cancellation between the main terms from
norms of bα and valuations of Pα (f, g).
     The leading terms in these dimensions also have a geometric meaning.
Hirzebruch’s proportionality principle related characteristic numbers of
compact locally symmetric spaces to those of their compact duals. Mum-
ford [Mum77] extended this comparison to noncompact arithmetic quotients
by controlling Hermitian metrics near the boundary. In [Alp26], the resulting
relation between intersection numbers and invariant volumes identifies the
leading growth of the dimensions of spaces of automorphic forms with a
polynomial in the highest weights of the representations at infinity. The inter-
section numbers used to bound covolumes in Section 4.3 thus also determine
the leading terms in these dimensions.

      5. Controlling denominators with Galois cohomology
   The term L in Equation 4.10 allows for the decrease in mean slope when
[Alp26] restricts and rescales the initial lattice of automorphic forms to record
divisibility of the period values. One difficulty in bounding that decrease
comes from projection onto chosen Hecke eigenspaces. A decomposition of a
vector space need not split an integral lattice inside it, so projection onto a
summand can introduce denominators even when the decomposition respects
every Hecke operator.
   To bound these denominators, [Alp26] uses integral operators that separate
the chosen Hecke eigenspaces from the other eigenspaces. The elementary
step is recorded in Lemma 5.1; the arithmetic enters in the divisibility
of the operators’ eigenvalues. The divisibility of these eigenvalues reflects
congruences between automorphic forms. To measure those congruences,
[Alp26] passes to extension classes of the associated Galois representations.
The bounds for the orders of the groups of extension classes retain their

dependence on the number fields, weights, and representations, since all three
change in the construction of the periods.

The connection with cohomology already appears in Mazur’s [Maz89, §1.6]
study of deformations. For the basic calculation, let F be a number field, let

of ramification, and let k be a finite field of characteristic ℓ. Write GF,S for

continuous residual representation ρ : GF,S −→ GLn (k), the adjoint module
adρ is Endk (kn ) with action g · X := ρ(g)Xρ(g)−1 .
    A lift to k[ε]/(ε2 ) has the form

The homomorphism condition makes c a 1-cocycle, and conjugation by

complete-intersection property of the Hecke algebras by varying auxiliary
primes. Thus the attempt to show that Galois representations come from
modular forms also gave a method for relating the size of a cohomology
group to congruences between modular forms.

   Before applying Lemma 6.1, [Alp26] bounds the terms in the expansion of
Atot − Mtot from characters retained by the averaging over H0 in Section 3
but not used in Gψ . For the choices in [Alp26], the sum of the absolute values
of these additional contributions is at most 4rx1/2+ε , where r is the order of
ψ. The following proposition records the uniform choices in [Alp26], with
the term 4rx1/2+ε subtracted in the definition of Z.
Proposition 6.3. Let ψ be a primitive Dirichlet character of order r. There
is C ≥ 0 such that, for every sufficiently small ε > 0, there are arbitrarily
large Y ≥ 3 for which the hypotheses of Lemma 6.1 can be satisfied at every
x ∈ [Y, Y 3/2 ] with this C and
                             Z = Gψ (x) − 4rx1/2+ε .
   The constant C in Proposition 6.3 accounts for the errors in the estimates
for period values and in the comparisons between integral lattices of auto-
morphic forms. It is fixed before ε is chosen; the remaining parameters may
depend on x. This order of choices allows the exponent in Lemma 6.1 to
approach 1/2.
Proof of Proposition 1.2. Fix ψ, η > 0, and A ≥ 2. Let r be the order of
ψ, and choose C as in Proposition 6.3. Choose ε > 0 small enough that
70                                  ROBIN ZHANG

the proposition applies and (C + 4)ε ≤ η. Since Proposition 6.3 permits
arbitrarily large Y, choose one with
                      Y ≥ max A, 3, 268/(3ε) , (1 + 4r)1/ε .

For each x ∈ [Y, Y 3/2 ], choose the parameters in Proposition 6.3 with the
same C. The choice of Y ensures that 26 ≤ x3ε/8 , so Lemma 6.1 gives
                         Gψ (x) ≤ x1/2+(C+3)ε + 4rx1/2+ε .
Since C + 3 ≥ 1 and 1 + 4r ≤ xε , it follows that
              Gψ (x) ≤ (1 + 4r)x1/2+(C+3)ε ≤ x1/2+(C+4)ε ≤ x1/2+η .
This holds throughout the chosen interval, with Y ≥ A.                               □
   With the interval bound of Proposition 1.2 in place, [Alp26] applies
Proposition 2.3 to exclude zeros of the Dirichlet L-functions to the right of the
critical line. The functional equation then excludes zeros in 0 < Re(s) < 1/2,
completing the proposed argument for Conjecture 1.1.
